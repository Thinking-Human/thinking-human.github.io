Diffusion models [1, 2] and flow matching models [3, 4] both generate samples by transforming a simple distribution into a data distribution. Although their training objectives look different, both can be understood through time-dependent vector fields.

An interesting mathematical question is: **Must these vector fields be gradients of scalar potentials?**

The answer reveals an important distinction between estimating a probability distribution and transporting it.

## Diffusion Scores are Gradient Fields

In score-based diffusion models, the central quantity is the score of a time-dependent probability density $p_t(x)$:

$$
s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x).
$$

Throughout, $\nabla$ and $\nabla\cdot$ act on $x$, so the subscript is dropped from here on. Defining the potential

$$
U_t(x)=-\log p_t(x),
$$

we immediately obtain

$$
s_t(x)=-\nabla U_t(x).
$$

Thus, the ideal score is a **conservative (curl-free) vector field**. Equivalently, its Jacobian is symmetric:

$$
\frac{\partial (s_t)_i}{\partial x_j}
=
\frac{\partial (s_t)_j}{\partial x_i}
\quad\text{for all } i,j.
$$

This property comes from the mathematical definition of the score, rather than the neural network architecture. A learned score network does not necessarily satisfy it exactly.

Importantly, the score should not be confused with the velocity of the diffusion probability-flow ODE [2]. The latter also includes the drift of the forward diffusion process and is not necessarily conservative.

## Flow Matching Learns Probability Transport

Flow matching instead learns a velocity field $v_t(x)$ defining an ODE:

$$
\frac{dx_t}{dt}=v_t(x_t).
$$

The evolving probability density must satisfy the **continuity equation**:

$$
\boxed{
\partial_t p_t+
\nabla\cdot(p_t v_t)=0
}
$$

This equation expresses conservation of probability mass. Unlike the definition of a score, it does not require $v_t$ to be the gradient of a scalar potential.

To see why, suppose $v_t$ satisfies the continuity equation. Consider another velocity field

$$
\tilde v_t=v_t+w_t,
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot(p_t w_t)=0
}
$$

Then

$$
\nabla\cdot(p_t\tilde v_t)
=
\nabla\cdot(p_t v_t).
$$

Consequently, both velocity fields generate the same density evolution, assuming the same initial density and suitable regularity conditions.

The additional field $w_t$ changes individual particle trajectories without changing the marginal probability distribution at any time.

This is a form of **gauge freedom in probability transport**.

## Decomposing a Probability Flow

The non-uniqueness suggests decomposing a velocity field into two components:

$$
\boxed{
v_t=\nabla\phi_t+w_t,
\qquad
\nabla\cdot(p_t w_t)=0
}
$$

This is a weighted Helmholtz decomposition, under appropriate regularity and boundary conditions. Horvat and Pfister [5] use the same kind of decomposition for diffusion models and show that a learned vector field need not be conservative: exact sampling and density estimation only require its conservative component to equal the true score.

The first component is a potential flow, while the second is weighted-divergence-free: it does not contribute to the instantaneous evolution of the probability density.

Using the weighted inner product

$$
\langle a,b\rangle_{p_t}
=
\int p_t(x)a(x)^\top b(x)\,dx,
\qquad
\|a\|_{p_t}^2=\langle a,a\rangle_{p_t},
$$

the two components are orthogonal:

$$
\langle\nabla\phi_t,w_t\rangle_{p_t}=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\|v_t\|_{p_t}^2
=
\|\nabla\phi_t\|_{p_t}^2
+
\|w_t\|_{p_t}^2
}
$$

This decomposition has a useful geometric interpretation: among velocity fields inducing the same instantaneous density change, the potential component is the minimum-norm representative.

It is important that the condition is **weighted divergence-free**, not simply $\nabla\cdot w_t=0$. Moreover, the decomposition is not equivalent to separating a field into its curl-free and rotational components in the ordinary unweighted sense.

## Connection to Optimal Transport

This structure is closely related to the dynamic formulation of Wasserstein-2 optimal transport [6]:

$$
W_2^2(p_0,p_1)
=
\inf_{p_t,v_t}
\int_0^1
\int p_t(x)\|v_t(x)\|^2\,dx\,dt,
$$

subject to the continuity equation and fixed endpoint distributions.

The objective minimizes the total kinetic action of probability transport. Under standard assumptions, an optimal velocity field can be represented as

$$
v_t=\nabla\phi_t.
$$

Thus, optimal transport selects a potential flow through a minimum-action principle.

**Important Note:** flow matching does not inherently produce non-conservative fields. For example, the common Gaussian path $x_t=\alpha_t x_1+\sigma_t\varepsilon$, with data $x_1$ and noise $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ drawn independently, yields a marginal velocity of the form

$$
v_t(x)=a_t x+b_t\nabla\log p_t(x),
\qquad
a_t=\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t},
\quad
b_t=\sigma_t\Big(\frac{\dot\alpha_t\sigma_t}{\alpha_t}-\dot\sigma_t\Big),
$$

where $a_t,b_t$ depend only on time. This velocity is itself a gradient field, of $\tfrac{a_t}{2}\|x\|^2+b_t\log p_t(x)$.

More general conditional paths and couplings, however, need not share this property.

## Takeaway

The distinction is not that diffusion always produces gradient flows while flow matching produces rotational flows.

Rather:

- **Score matching** estimates a vector field that is a gradient by definition.
- **Flow matching** estimates a velocity field satisfying probability conservation, which does not generally require a potential representation.
- **Weighted Helmholtz decomposition** separates density-changing transport from components that preserve the instantaneous density.
- **Optimal transport** provides a geometric principle for selecting a minimum-action representative.

In summary, the broader insight is that **the evolution of a probability distribution does not uniquely determine how individual particles move through space**.
